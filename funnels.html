How the City went about identifying pieds-à-terre, and what it should have done instead

The surcharge applies only to homes that are not the owner's primary residence. Switch between the Department of Finance's actual process and the process recommended in the accompanying essay.

How the City narrowed the list

Finance published every property in New York City under the banner of the surcharge, then mailed about 17,000 letters saying the property "may be subject" to the tax. None says why it arrived.

Step 1 · What Finance published

900,000+

Properties on the public list

A roll of essentially every property in the city, released under the banner of a tax almost none of them owe.

Step 2 · Value screen

≈24,000

Clear the value thresholds

Houses valued at $5 million or more; condos and co-ops at $1 million or more.

Step 3 · Partial residency screen

≈17,000

Warning letters mailed

Several thousand properties were pulled out first — almost certainly ones Finance's own records already flag as primary residences.

Step 4 · The burden shifts

Every recipient starts from scratch

Burden of proof: the owner

The letter names no missing document and gives no reason it arrived — including to owners Finance had already accepted as residents.

What it produced

Thousands of letters went to owners Finance had already screened out, and the public list was broad enough to include the finance commissioner's own home and a former mayor's — both well under the threshold.

How the City could narrow the list

Two screens run on data Finance already holds or can lawfully obtain would clear the large majority of these owners without asking them for anything. Only the genuinely ambiguous cases get a letter — and that letter says why.

Start here · Value screen

≈24,000

Properties over the thresholds

Publish this list, not the 900,000, with a plain caveat: clearing the value line is not the same as owing the tax.

Screen one · What Finance already knows

≈14,000

Author's estimate

Clear every owner Finance has already accepted as an occupant: the yearly owner-occupancy certification behind the co-op/condo abatement, plus the senior, veteran and STAR exemptions.

Screen two · What the State can confirm

The large majority cleared

No published figure

The surcharge test is the address on the New York State income tax return. Finance doesn't hold it, the State does, and the statute lets the two departments share it.

Screen three · Who is left

The ambiguous cases

Homes held by an LLC or in trust, rented out, or occupied by a family member. Finance knows which signal was missing for each, so the letter can name it and say exactly what to send.

What it would produce

Far fewer letters, and each one a set of instructions rather than a warning. Value stays public, residency screening stays private, and the City still collects the roughly $500 million projected for this year.

Figures and how to read them

All figures are as reported in Martha E. Stark's accompanying essay: the published roll of more than 900,000 properties, roughly 24,000 properties above the value thresholds, about 17,000 letters mailed, and the roughly $500 million in revenue projected for this year.

Two numbers are not agency figures and are labeled as such. The gap between 24,000 and 17,000 is Stark's inference that Finance ran a partial residency screen; the agency has not published its screening criteria. The roughly 14,000 remaining after a full screen of Finance's own records is explicitly the author's estimate. The share cleared by a State income tax data match is described in the essay as "the large majority" and carries no published figure.

Band widths are illustrative, not drawn to scale. A true-to-scale funnel starting at 900,000 would render every later stage invisible.
